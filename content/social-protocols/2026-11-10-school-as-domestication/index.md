Hunter-gatherers fought hard to keep anyone from looking like a boss. Factories and offices need the opposite: show up on time, sit or stand where you are told, and take a ranking without walking out. The missing decade between those two instincts is school.

### The bathroom pass is the curriculum

A child who needs the bathroom has to ask first. The wait teaches that the body's needs run through an adult's permission, and that lesson outlasts the hallway. Teachers rarely name it as a lesson, but they keep rewarding the children who wait without complaint.

### Unschooled workers would not tend the extra machine

Managers saw this on the factory floor. Cotton-mill **doffers** (workers who pull full spools off spinning machines) did the same job on the same machines in 1910, and productivity still varied by a factor of six. In some regions each doffer ran six machines; in others, one. The bottleneck was the workers: in less industrialized places, they **refused to do as they were told**.

An American visitor to India in the 1920s said the workers could easily have tended more machines, and they would not. Managers offered exhortation, ambition, and extra pay, and the workers still held back. When Bombay mill managers tried to raise machines per worker in 1928, the workers went on strike. Workers in Europe and Latin America refused in similar ways: they arrived late, clung to superstition, preferred **hints over direct orders**, and turned down tasks that clashed with status among coworkers.

Later managers called the fix a **modern upbringing**, and schooling was how they got one. Without it, managers could not count on people to follow orders.

### You unlearn equal shares

Most fifth graders still want to divide things equally. By late adolescence, most prefer to divide in proportion to **individual achievement**. Teachers teach subjects, and they also teach a **meritocratic ethos**, so students come to see ranking as fair.

### We trade curiosity for manners

This kind of domestication is easier to celebrate: less violence, more politeness, more cooperation. In historian Eugene Weber's account, the French state used schools to **civilize** peasants: hygiene, greetings, knocking on doors, how to behave in decent company. Where the lessons did not take, he wrote, people stayed coarse and kept brawling.

The trade-off is mixed. Teachers prepare you for the workplace, and maybe for a quieter street, but they have to **break your spirit** and train you to submit to a modern hierarchy. The benefits are real.

Einstein put the cost in one line: it is a miracle that modern instruction has not yet entirely strangled the **holy curiosity of inquiry**.

### What they said

> Children are expected to sit still for hours upon hours; to control their impulses; to focus on boring, repetitive tasks; to move from place to place when a bell rings; and even to ask permission before going to the bathroom (think about that for a second). Teachers systematically reward children for being docile and punish them for "acting out," that is, for acting as their own masters. In fact, teachers reward discipline independent of its influence on learning, and in ways that tamp down on student creativity. Children are also trained to accept being measured, graded, and ranked, often in front of others. This enterprise, which typically lasts well over a decade, serves as a systematic exercise in human domestication.
>
> (Kevin Simler and Robin Hanson, *The Elephant in the Brain*, 2018, Chapter 13)
