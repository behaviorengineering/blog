J. P. Morgan gets credited with a blunt line: a man has two reasons for doing something, a good reason and the real reason. The attribution arrives late (Owen Wister, 1930), and the saying was already loose in the air.

### The good reason is for the room

When you quit a job, take a deal, or pick a side in an argument, you usually lead with the version that sounds fair, practical, or kind. That **acceptable reason** travels well: it lowers conflict, protects reputation, and gives other people a story they can nod at without digging.

That outward story works like a **deniability hood**: a clean cover that blocks anyone from pinning the real motive. At the group level, the same hood keeps shared suspicion from becoming shared certainty. Inside one skull, it lets you keep acting while the public account stays respectable.

### The real reason can hide from you too

The deeper driver is often messier: status, spite, fear of looking small, a private hunger for control, relief, belonging. Sometimes you know that and still lead with the good reason. Sometimes the motive stays foggy even to you. The mind softens, rearranges, or buries the motive that would bruise self-image.

Psychologists call this the **cold-hot empathy gap**: when you are calm, you cannot really feel what you will want when you are angry, afraid, or desperate, and vice versa.

You can lie to the room on purpose. You can also buy your own story. Either way, the real push keeps driving, while the clean why is what you can say out loud.

### Check both sides

When someone hands you a clean why, including when that someone is you, check both sides. What does the good reason shield in public? What does the real reason want when nobody is watching?
