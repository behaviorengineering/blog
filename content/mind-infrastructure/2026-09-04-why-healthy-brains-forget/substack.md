## Why does a healthy brain forget your keys?

Everyday **forgetting** (lost keys, a blank name) is the brain dropping what it never **encoded**: you never paid enough **attention** to store it. It keeps what is meaningful, emotional, surprising, or repeated.

Most of those lapses come from **distraction** at *encoding*, the moment attention becomes a memory. You cannot retrieve a parking spot you never looked at.

> We as human beings have to accept a certain amount of forgetting in our lives; it is totally normal.

## Even a memory champion forgets a birthday

A memory champion, Akira Haraguchi, memorized more than 111,000 digits of pi and still forgot a birthday. Human memory was never built to catalog every name, later task, or object you pass.

Recall **replays** a pattern of neural activity across the brain. The **hippocampus** (the region that weaves new memories) binds the pieces into one circuit. Formation has four steps: **encoding**, **consolidation** in sleep, **storage**, and **retrieval**.

**Episodic** memory (what happened to you) can change each time you recall it. **Prospective** memory, remembering to do something later, fails by design. Pilots lower the wheels with a checklist.

## Names vanish; later tasks need a checklist

Names, movie titles, and cities are abstract proper nouns: few routes in. A baker's job (apron, oven, smell) has thousands. That is the baker/Baker paradox: the occupation sticks; the surname does not.

Phones and feeds steal the second of **attention** that encoding needs. Googling a missing word leaves the store intact. **Digital amnesia** (the idea that search tools weaken memory) is a separate fear. Search is an *open-book test*: you retrieve the word and keep thinking. The leak is the stolen second, not the search.

## Alzheimer's fails new memories, not lost keys

**Alzheimer's** starts in the hippocampus and can run silently for 15 to 20 years. Normal forgetting is misplacing keys. The early disease picture is failing to form new memories and not knowing how you arrived.

For about 98% of people, inherited genes plus lifestyle decide whether **amyloid plaques** (sticky protein clumps) reach a tipping point. About 2% of cases are purely genetic.

Sleep lets **glial cells** (support cells that clear debris) wash out extra amyloid. Aerobic walking, a Mediterranean or MIND diet, new learning, and unmanaged **chronic stress** are the levers most people actually have. Learning builds **cognitive reserve**: extra neural routes so pathology can be present and function can last longer.

Everyday forgetting tracks **attention**. Alzheimer's tracks a slow **plaque clock**: years of sticky protein buildup that starts in the hippocampus.
