# Reptilocracy Characters and Species Guide

LOAD-WHEN: pitching or casting Reptilocracy jokes under skill `site-reptilocracy-themes`.

## Purpose

This document defines how species function symbolically inside the
Reptilocracy world.

It is not a rigid casting rulebook.
It is a guide for keeping character use consistent across jokes,
prompts, cartoons, and explanations.

The goal is not biological accuracy.
The goal is symbolic clarity.

## Core Principle

In Reptilocracy, species are not random decoration.
They are part of the argument.

The chosen creature should help the viewer understand, very quickly:

- who has power
- what kind of power it is
- how that power behaves
- who is exposed to it

Each species brings a different emotional and symbolic flavour.
Use that flavour deliberately.

## General Casting Rules

### Reptiles

Reptiles usually represent institutions, elite actors, systems of
control, public authority, managerial classes, propaganda operators,
or insulated decision-makers.

They often appear as:

- politicians
- ministers
- bureaucrats
- editors
- spokespeople
- security actors
- consultants
- experts
- administrators
- managers

They should usually keep natural animal bodies.
Do not rely on full human clothing to signal status.

Small accessories are allowed when useful, such as:

- clipboards
- headsets
- mugs
- styluses
- microphones
- badges
- ID cards
- folders
- pointers

### Insects and smaller creatures

Smaller creatures usually represent the public and the consequences of
institutional behaviour.

They often appear as:

- citizens
- workers
- patients
- reporters
- users
- voters
- families
- bystanders
- casualties
- data points

They are useful because they quickly communicate scale,
collectivity, vulnerability, and disposability.

## Species Profiles

## Frogs

### Core read

Frogs are the default Reptilocracy power animal.

They usually read as:

- managerial
- procedural
- media-trained
- committee-minded
- softly spoken
- complacent
- self-protective
- calm in the face of visible harm

### Best use cases

Use frogs for:

- ministers
- parliamentary figures
- officials
- bureaucratic spokespeople
- presenters
- administrators
- people who normalize the absurd
- those who sound reasonable while defending something rotten

### Visual behaviour

Frogs often work best when:

- standing beside podiums
- gesturing at dashboards
- presenting metrics
- calmly narrating system failure
- remaining dry while others are soaked, crushed, queued, or ignored

### Tone effect

A frog makes the panel feel more bureaucratic than overtly violent.
It is useful when the satire is about soft power, institutional tone,
PR, process language, or civilised cruelty.

## Lizards

### Core read

Lizards read as sharper, drier, more tactical, and more openly alert
than frogs.

They usually suggest:

- strategic thinking
- self-preservation
- opportunism
- cynical adaptation
- editorial or operational control
- behind-the-scenes management

### Best use cases

Use lizards for:

- operators
- advisors
- tacticians
- editors
- party fixers
- policy technicians
- cynical institutional actors
- people who know exactly what they are doing

### Visual behaviour

Lizards work well when:

- running consoles
- editing metrics
- moving levers
- steering a machine
- watching others from the side
- doing the active manipulation while frogs explain it

### Tone effect

A lizard introduces more deliberate calculation.
It is useful when the joke is about selective pressure,
message discipline, political management, or targeted harm.

## Komodo dragons

### Core read

Komodos read as heavy, dominant, physically threatening, and quietly
predatory.

They suggest:

- hard authority
- coercion
- domination
- blunt force inside a formal setting
- moral danger disguised as legitimacy

### Best use cases

Use komodos for:

- strongman figures
- enforcers
- “values” instructors
- hard-power ministers
- brutal institutional certainty
- elite actors whose violence is obvious but normalized

### Visual behaviour

Komodos work well when:

- towering over smaller creatures
- teaching or lecturing from the front of a room
- occupying the centre of a stage
- blocking exits
- standing in a beam of authority light

### Tone effect

A komodo makes the scene feel more openly menacing.
Use it when you want the threat to be legible even before the text is
read.

## Crocodiles and alligators

### Core read

Crocodilian reptiles read as older, colder, heavier institutions.
They imply force that is ancient, patient, and structurally protected.

They suggest:

- state violence
- punishment
- security logic
- disciplined cruelty
- predatory patience
- durable old power

### Best use cases

Use crocodiles or alligators for:

- police power
- border regimes
- security systems
- prison logic
- militarized administration
- visibly dangerous state authority

### Visual behaviour

They work well when:

- half-submerged in a system or structure
- guarding thresholds
- occupying checkpoints
- waiting at the bottom of pits or channels
- acting as the thing a pipeline feeds into

### Tone effect

They are stronger than frogs and colder than komodos.
Use them when power should feel less theatrical and more structurally
predatory.

## Chameleons

### Core read

Chameleons represent adaptation in the service of power.
They are useful for showing political flexibility without principle.

They usually suggest:

- rebranding
- PR
- spin
- consultancy language
- moral camouflage
- rhetorical shape-shifting
- institutional reinvention without change

### Best use cases

Use chameleons for:

- consultants
- communicators
- public relations figures
- strategic branding exercises
- politicians changing message for audience or context
- spectacle posing as responsiveness

### Visual behaviour

Chameleons work well when:

- blending into a background or sign
- changing colour to match branding
- emerging from clown or performance imagery
- standing beside slogans that contradict reality
- appearing to fit anywhere while meaning nothing

### Tone effect

A chameleon makes the joke more about image management than direct force.
Use it when the target is adaptive hypocrisy.

## Snakes

### Core read

Snakes suggest stealth, whispering influence, insinuation,
coercive persuasion, and concentrated threat.

They often imply:

- backroom pressure
- ideological poison
- manipulation by suggestion
- quiet menace
- seduction into wrongness

### Best use cases

Use snakes for:

- lobbyists
- whisperers
- ideologues
- operators of fear
- disinformation themes
- selective intimidation

### Visual behaviour

Snakes work well when:

- coiled around podiums, microphones, or funding pipes
- leaning into ears
- threading through walls or systems
- acting as a line of influence rather than a body in the open

### Tone effect

A snake makes the scene feel more secretive and venomous.
Use it when power should feel intimate, infiltrating, and corrupting.

## Ants

### Core read

Ants are the core public species in Reptilocracy.

They usually represent:

- ordinary people
- workers
- patients
- voters
- families
- the governed
- those counted but not heard
- those structurally burdened by policy

### Why ants work

Ants communicate several things at once:

- small scale against big systems
- collective life
- labour
- endurance
- replaceability in the eyes of power
- queue logic and crowd logic

### Best use cases

Use ants for:

- queues
- crowds
- data points
- witnesses
- struggling individuals inside giant systems
- people reduced to traffic, metrics, or backlog

### Visual behaviour

Ants work well when:

- clustered below platforms
- funneled through systems
- sitting alone under giant interfaces
- carrying paperwork, buckets, or votes
- looking up at something much larger than themselves

### Tone effect

Ants keep the satire grounded.
They give scale and consequence.
They are often the emotional anchor of the image.

## Flies

### Core read

Flies suggest irritation, neglect, media noise, contamination,
and things gathering around decay.

They can represent:

- low-grade media attention
- nuisance reporting
- scandal swarms
- unwanted witnesses
- public rot made visible

### Best use cases

Use flies for:

- press swarms
- scandal scenes
- decay around institutions
- attention that is annoying but not transformative

### Tone effect

Flies are good when the scene needs noise or public irritation rather
than grounded human consequence.
Use ants when you need solidarity or burden.
Use flies when you need nuisance, swarm, or rot.

## Other small creatures

Other insects or small animals can be used occasionally, but they should
have a clear reason.

Examples:

- beetles for hard-shelled functionaries or stubborn process
- moths for fatal attraction to spectacle or flame
- termites for hidden institutional erosion
- roaches for survivability inside rot

Use these sparingly so the world does not become visually random.

## Power Relationship Patterns

These patterns help keep species use consistent.

### Frog + ants

Best for:

- bureaucracy versus public consequence
- official language versus lived harm
- procedure versus need

### Lizard + ants

Best for:

- manipulation
- strategic cruelty
- selective enforcement
- data smoothing

### Komodo + ants

Best for:

- intimidation
- values enforcement
- hard authoritarian lessons
- normalized aggression

### Chameleon + ants

Best for:

- spin
- branding
- fake responsiveness
- cosmetic reform

### Frog + lizard

Useful when splitting roles between:

- public explanation and backroom manipulation
- spokesperson and operator
- procedure and tactical execution

### Reptile group + ants

Useful for scenes involving:

- committees
- cabinets
- editorial boards
- ruling classes
- managerial consensus against the public

## Character Function Types

Regardless of species, most characters in Reptilocracy fall into one of a
few functional roles.

### The presenter

Stands beside the mechanism and explains the absurd calmly.
Usually a frog.

### The operator

Actively manipulates a control, lever, graph, switch, or flow.
Usually a lizard, frog, or chameleon.

### The enforcer

Embodies force, threat, or dominance.
Usually a komodo, crocodile, or large lizard.

### The witness

Small creature who sees the contradiction.
Usually an ant.

### The crowd

Collective consequence, backlog, exposed population, or data mass.
Usually ants.

### The decorative victim of abstraction

A creature reduced to an icon, dot, label, or queue position while the
real system looks elsewhere.
Usually an ant or small insect.

## Behaviour Rules

Species should be expressed through:

- posture
- placement
- gaze
- scale
- behaviour
- relationship to objects and systems

Do not overperform species differences.
The point is symbolic clarity, not cartoon zoology.

Keep behaviour controlled.
The tone is stronger when the creatures act as if the monstrous system is
ordinary.

## Repetition and Variety

Frogs and ants are the core visual grammar of the series.
That is useful.
Do not avoid them just to create variety.

Add other species when they sharpen meaning.
A new species should change the flavour of power, not just the silhouette.

## Avoid

Avoid using species randomly.
Avoid making all reptiles interchangeable.
Avoid using too many species in one panel unless the distinction matters.
Avoid cute or zoological detail that distracts from institutional meaning.
Avoid assigning a species with no symbolic payoff.

## Quick Reference

| Species | Main meaning | Best for |

| Frog | bureaucracy, calm officialdom, procedural absurdity | ministers, presenters, officials |
| Lizard | tactical control, cynical management, manipulation | operators, advisors, editors |
| Komodo | dominance, menace, hard power | enforcers, strongmen, harsh authority |
| Crocodile / alligator | structural predation, security logic, punishment | police, borders, prisons, force systems |
| Chameleon | PR, adaptation, rebranding, spin | consultants, image managers, opportunists |
| Snake | whisper power, poison, covert influence | lobbyists, ideologues, hidden coercion |
| Ant | public burden, workers, citizens, consequences | queues, patients, voters, witnesses |
| Fly | nuisance attention, decay, media swarm | scandal, rot, noise, annoyance |

## Final Rule

Pick the species that makes the joke faster to understand.

If two species could work, choose the one that best sharpens the type of
power being shown.

In Reptilocracy, the species choice is part of the satire.
