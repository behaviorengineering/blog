Everyone knows, nobody acts

You can see the cheat from your desk and still do nothing. Your counterpart on another team saw it too. Neither of you has said it out loud, so moving first looks paranoid, rude, or like a career error.

Groups do not punish on private doubt. They punish when everyone knows the cheat, and each person can see that the others know it too. That nested certainty is common knowledge. Without it, private seeing stays stuck.

A public trial works because the room sees that the room got the same message. Hallway gossip leaves you unsure your version matches theirs. People keep nodding along until someone speaks where others can hear that others heard.

Once the company gets big, information climbs and detail dies, so thinking stops at your layer. A con artist only needs each person to think, "Maybe I am the only one who sees this." Sideways talk is thin.

Ten people saw the problem in a cross-team meeting. The VP got one skip-level bullet, trimmed by a director. The cheat sits in what got cut.

In a pyramid, the speech that could flip the room often dies at the next layer. Going first can cost your reputation, your standing, and your relationships. Without a message the whole room hears together, the cheat keeps operating.
