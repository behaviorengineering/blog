The Prisoner's Dilemma, Tit for Tat, and how cooperation scales

In the Prisoner's Dilemma, two players who each chase a private gain can both end up worse off. But real life is rarely a one-off game.

When interactions are repeated (impalas grooming, nations negotiating, roommates doing chores), the logic flips: reciprocity becomes the most profitable strategy.

Axelrod ran a tournament of computer programs to prove this. The winning strategy was Tit for Tat (cooperate first, then mirror the last move). It dominated because it had four key traits:

✜ Nice: Never defects first. Systematic nastiness destroys the possibility of mutual gain.
✜ Forgiving: Retaliates, but does not hold long grudges.
✜ Retaliatory: Punishes betrayal immediately so it is not a pushover.
✜ Clear: It is simple and easy to read.

But strict Tit for Tat fails in the real world. To survive mistakes, you need generosity: forgiving occasionally to restore cooperation.

Many people assume winning means beating the other player. But you often win not by defeating others, but by helping create an environment where mutual cooperation is stable.
