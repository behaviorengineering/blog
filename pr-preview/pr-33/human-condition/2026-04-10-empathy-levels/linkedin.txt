🫂🧱 Empathy is a stack of feeling, perspective, and judgment, and systems can aim it

Did you know when we ask young kids to share toys, they often cannot fathom why we ask them to do it?  👀

To them, the toy is theirs, the feeling is obvious, and the rule feels arbitrary. Empathy is mostly learned, with brain maturity.

✜ Emotional empathy →
    - I feel what you feel (or what the herd is feeling).
    - Seeing someone get hurt makes your stomach clench.
    - Tension in the house when people get angry makes your whole body tighten, even before anyone explains why.

✜ Cognitive empathy →
    - I see what you see, I can track your point of view.
    - I can understand your reasons, even if I do not share your feelings.

✜ Moral‑cognitive empathy →
    - I know you could feel guilty even when you win, and I use that inner state to judge you.

ℹ Most kids do not start with an inner judge.
At 4 or 5, if you ask them how a kid feels after breaking a rule and getting what they wanted, many will say he feels good, because the outcome was good.
"Wrong" is still about "did it work?" not "what kind of person does this make me?".

Somewhere around 7–8, the simulation upgrades.
Now they expect a wrongdoer to feel bad even when they got the prize, and they rate a happy wrongdoer as worse than a sorry one. They are not just reading faces anymore. They are running a model of an internal courtroom, with guilt as a real state that matters.

On top of that sits social or compassionate empathy:
You move from "I get you" to "I will act on this". You share, protect, or intervene not just because you feel it, but because "people like us do not do that to each other".

A lot of adults never fully stabilise that third layer: They can copy the right expressions, say the right words, and play the remorse script on cue.

"Under the hood, they are still running outcome‑centric code."

And systems can hijack that! They do not have to remove empathy. They can overwrite your earlier layers by dehumanising or vilifying others, especially the ones who should be judged.

Once that happens, your moral‑cognitive empathy stops asking "should we feel guilty for this?" and starts asking "what should happen to them?".

Same feelings.
Different target.
Very different behaviour.
